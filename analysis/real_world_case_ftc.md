Real-World Phishing Case Study: FTC Example

1. Source Information

- Source: Federal Trade Commission (FTC), United States
- Source type: Official consumer-protection guidance
- Reference: "How to Recognize and Avoid Phishing Scams" (https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/how-recognize-and-avoid-phishing-scams)
- Case type: Real-world phishing email example published by the FTC

2. Case Overview

The FTC presents an example of a phishing email that appears to come from a familiar company. The message claims that the recipient's account is on hold because of a billing problem and encourages the recipient to follow a link to update payment information.

The example demonstrates how attackers can imitate trusted organizations to make fraudulent messages appear legitimate.

3. Identified Phishing Indicators

Indicator| Explanation
Generic greeting| The message does not use a specific personal greeting.
Account-related urgency| It claims that the recipient's account is on hold.
Billing problem| It creates concern about a payment issue.
Payment-update link| It encourages the recipient to follow a link and provide payment information.
Brand impersonation| Familiar branding and a company logo can make the message appear trustworthy.

4. Classification

Classification: Phishing

The FTC identifies the example as a scam. The message uses a billing-related concern and a payment-update link to encourage the recipient to take action.

5. Risk Assessment

- Financial risk: The recipient may disclose payment information.
- Privacy risk: Personal or account information could be exposed.
- Account security risk: Information provided through a fraudulent page could be misused.

6. Recommended Prevention Measures

1. Do not click unexpected payment or account-verification links.
2. Visit the organization's official website directly.
3. Contact the organization using a verified phone number or official support channel.
4. Check unexpected billing notifications independently.
5. Avoid sharing passwords, payment details, or verification codes through email links.

7. Evidence Limitations

The FTC webpage provides an illustrative phishing email example, but it does not provide the original raw email or its complete technical headers.

Therefore, this case study does not claim to have verified:

- The actual sender address or sending domain.
- SPF, DKIM, or DMARC authentication results.
- The original email's routing information.
- The destination URL or its technical reputation.

These details are marked as unavailable rather than assumed.

8. Conclusion

This case demonstrates how phishing messages exploit trust, urgency, and billing-related concerns. Users should independently verify unexpected account notifications instead of relying on links included in suspicious emails.
